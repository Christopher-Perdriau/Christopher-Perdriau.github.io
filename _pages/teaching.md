## Instructor of Record — Accessibility, Technology, and Society

**University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, Fall 2026**

I designed this course from scratch, developing 30+ original lectures and assignments on models of disability, technoableism, and accessibility law.

The course moves from foundational frameworks — the medical model versus the social model of disability, disability law (IDEA), technoableism, and disability justice — into applied, technical accessibility topics: assistive technology, screen readers, semantic HTML and the accessibility tree, ARIA roles/states/properties, WCAG, color and contrast, and the intersections of accessibility with security and with AI. The course closes with student group presentations and a session reimagining computer science from an access-centered perspective. The reading list draws on current HCI/accessibility/CS-education research (CHI, ASSETS, SIGCSE, and related venues, largely 2024–2026), rather than relying only on textbook material.

Assignments are scaffolded and recurring rather than a stack of one-off essays: short reflective writing tied to each week's reading, a running "Comparative Analysis" assignment that applies disability-theory frameworks (social model, medical model, technoableism, biopsychosocial model, affirmation model) to evaluate papers across multiple weeks, a WCAG evaluation assignment, an "Ability Heuristics Evaluation," and group presentations later in the term. An extra-credit option invites students to read a chapter from a trade book on disability and ableism — such as Ashley Shew's *Against Technoableism* or Jay Dolmage's *Academic Ableism* — and connect it to course material and their own experience.

## Teaching Philosophy

My approach is empathy-driven: I aim to build genuine personal connections with students, stay transparent about grading and expectations, and scaffold students' metacognition — for instance, by narrating my own reasoning, and my mistakes, out loud during live coding.

As a TA for an introductory CS course, I had one student in a lab section who was consistently disengaged — she'd sigh, slouch, copy answers from neighbors, and deflect any offer of help. Rather than pushing harder on course content, I shifted to building a personal connection over several weeks: asking about her major, whether she'd grown up in Illinois, her family. By the end of the semester she had stopped copying her neighbors and started helping them instead.

Some feedback from student evaluations:

> "Chris is such a warm and welcoming instructor. He really made me feel comfortable asking any questions I had and he created a positive environment conducive to learning."

> "Chris would ask questions to help us and engage us in the learning process."

> "Chris's greatest strength is his ability to work through the problems with you until you really understand it."
